# Avoiding Scams in Peer-to-Peer Trades

Most traders are honest people. But anywhere strangers exchange valuable items, scammers show up too. The good news: their tricks are old, well-known, and easy to spot once you know what to look for.

## The golden rule

**Never hand over your item — or your money — for something you haven't seen and checked yourself.** Almost every scam in peer-to-peer trading is a variation on getting you to break this rule. Meet in person, inspect the item, and complete the exchange on the spot.

## Scam patterns to know

**The overpayment trick.** They "accidentally" send too much money and ask you to refund the difference. The original payment is fake or stolen and gets reversed — but your refund was real. Rule: never refund overpayments. Cancel and walk away.

**The fake payment screenshot.** They show you a convincing screenshot of a bank transfer or payment app receipt. Screenshots prove nothing — they take ten seconds to fake. Only trust money you can see in your own account, and even then, wait for it to fully clear before handing anything over.

**The ship-it-first request.** They can't meet in person but promise to ship their item as soon as you ship yours. Your item arrives at their door; theirs never leaves. If you can't meet in person, don't do the trade. No exceptions for "great deals."

**The too-good-to-be-true listing.** A brand-new phone worth $900 offered straight across for your old $200 tablet? That's not generosity — it's bait. Scammers use impossible deals to make you act fast and skip your normal caution. If the value gap makes no sense, there's a reason.

**The escrow / middleman twist.** They suggest using a "safe escrow service" or a mutual friend to hold items — and they provide the service, which is fake, or the friend, who is in on it. Only use escrow services you found and verified yourself, and be skeptical of any third party the other side introduces.

**The stolen goods trap.** An item priced at half its value, no box, no accessories, vague answers about where it came from. Buying or trading for stolen goods can mean losing the item when it's recovered — and legal trouble. Ask where they got it. Honest sellers answer easily.

## Protect your information

Keep all communication on the trading platform's messaging system. Moving to personal email, text, or chat apps right away is a mild red flag — scammers prefer channels the platform can't see. Never share your home address, bank login, or ID documents. A trade needs none of those.

## Verify the person, not just the item

- Check their profile: how long have they been on the platform? Do they have completed trades or reviews?
- Be wary of brand-new accounts pushing high-value trades fast.
- A video call before meeting is reasonable for expensive items — scammers usually refuse.

## The meetup is your safety net

Every in-person safety rule doubles as scam protection: public place, daytime, bring a friend, inspect thoroughly. Most scams collapse the moment the scammer has to show up in person with the real item. That's exactly why they try so hard to avoid meeting.

Platforms built for trading help here too. On Valora, for instance, trade offers are formal — both sides agree to specific terms, items, and any cash on top before meeting — so there's a written record of what was promised. If what shows up doesn't match, you walk away with the record to back you up.

## If it happens anyway

Act fast: save every message and screenshot everything. Report the user on the platform. If money changed hands, contact your bank or payment provider immediately — reversals are time-sensitive. For significant amounts, file a police report; bring your records. And tell the platform community if there's a way to warn others.

## The short version

See the item in person before anything changes hands. Distrust overpayments, screenshots-as-proof, ship-first requests, and impossible deals. Keep chat on the platform, meet in public, and trust the feeling that says "this is off" — it's usually right.
