# How to Value Your Used Items Before a Trade

The hardest part of any trade is answering one question: what is my stuff actually worth? Get it right and trades happen fast. Get it wrong and your listing sits for months — or you give away value without realizing it. Here's how to find a fair number.

## Start with sold listings, not asking prices

Anyone can ask any price. What matters is what people actually paid. Search for your item on eBay and filter by "sold items," or browse Facebook Marketplace and similar sites for the same model. Write down 5–10 real sale prices. Throw out the highest and the lowest, and average the rest. That average is your starting point — the real market value, not someone's wishful thinking.

Do the same for the item you want in return. A trade is fair when both sides are close in real market value, with cash on top covering any gap.

## Condition is everything

Two identical items can be worth very different amounts depending on condition. Be honest with yourself:

- **Like new:** barely used, no scratches, everything included. Worth near the top of the range.
- **Good:** normal signs of use, works perfectly. This is where most items land.
- **Fair:** visible wear, maybe a small flaw, but fully working. Expect noticeably less.
- **For parts:** broken or incomplete. Worth a fraction — only useful to someone who repairs things.

When in doubt, rate your item one step lower than your first instinct. Under-promising and over-delivering builds trust; the reverse kills trades.

## Factor in brand and demand

Some brands hold value for years. A well-known tool brand, a popular camera maker, a bike brand people search for — these trade faster and hold higher value than no-name equivalents. On the other hand, fast-changing tech (phones, tablets, laptops) loses value quickly because a newer model is always around the corner.

Ask yourself: if I listed this for cash today, how many people would want it this week? High demand means you can hold firm on value. Low demand means you may need to accept less or add cash on top to make the trade attractive.

## Don't forget what's in the box

Original packaging, manuals, chargers, straps, extra batteries, and accessories all add value — sometimes 10–20% more. If you still have the receipt or proof of purchase, even better. Gather everything that came with the item before you photograph and list it.

## Age matters, but not how you think

"Old" doesn't automatically mean "worthless." A five-year-old quality tool can be worth more than a one-year-old cheap one. What matters is useful life remaining. A bike with fresh tires and a tuned drivetrain is worth more than the same bike neglected in a garage. If you've maintained it, say so — and show it in your photos.

## The cash-on-top bridge

Perfect value matches are rare. That's normal. If your item is worth about $200 and theirs is worth about $260, the trade still works — you add $60 on top. Agree on values first using the research above, then the cash difference is simple math instead of an argument. Most platforms built for trading, Valora included, let you include cash on top directly in the offer so the terms are clear before anyone meets.

## Write your reasoning in the listing

You don't need to publish a spreadsheet, but one honest line helps enormously: "Similar ones sell for around $220–$250; priced fairly for quick trade." It shows you've done your homework, and it stops low-ball offers before they start.

## The short version

Check real sold prices, grade your condition honestly, count your accessories, and let cash on top bridge small gaps. Fair value is what makes trades happen fast.
